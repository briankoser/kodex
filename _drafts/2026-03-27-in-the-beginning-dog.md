## Introduction
Philip Day Eastman was a protege of Dr. Seuss. Wrote in 1961. Worked on Private Snafu propaganda cartoons with Seuss. Eastman worked for Disney. GDG his best; one hit wonder.

All sources are footnoted, because I am a scholar.

## Page 1: Title
A page-by-page examination of *Go, Dog. Go!* reveals a structural marvel, a sort of cathedral built by one man in the space of 64 pages. Although printed by Random House, there is nothing random about it.

## Page 2: Dedication
Eastman dedicated his *bonum opus* "To Cluny". Some have claimed that Cluny was the name of Dr. Seuss's dog<sup><a id="backlink-1" aria-label="Footnote #1" href="#footnote-1">[1]</a></sup> That may be the meaning of the dedication on the historical level, the first and most base level of meaning.

On a higher level, Cluny is a reference to the Abbey of Cluny, the largest church in Christendom until the building of St. Peter's Basilica. One of the most important monasteries in medieval Europe, Cluny led in the establishment of Western monasticism. It's influence waned as the pope's waxed, and it finally ceased to be a church when French Revolutionaries fired the buildings (including the library) and torn down the walls for stone to rebuild the town. The buildings that remain today house an engineering school. <sup><a id="backlink-2" aria-label="Footnote #2" href="#footnote-2">[2]</a></sup>

But why dedicate *Go, Dog. Go!* to Cluny? Three possibilities emerge from the mists of Eastman's conscious, subconscious, and/or unconscious mind.

Perhaps while reading we should have in mind St. Odo of Cluny, the second abbot of Cluny, who is said to have invented the spoon. <sup><a id="backlink-3" aria-label="Footnote #3" href="#footnote-3">[3]</a></sup> The biggest argument against this theory is that the *Go, Dog. Go!* illustrations contain zero spoons.

Some evidence suggests that its a reference to Odilo of Cluny, the fifth Benedictine abbot of Cluny. He made Cluny into the most important monastery in Western Europe and established All Souls' Day. (source: This one was probably Wikipedia) In support of this theory, there is a paper lantern on the boat on Page 33; this may or may not have been lit for the departed dead.

But by far the most likely referent is the very founding of Cluny. When Duke William of Aquitaine (200 years before Eleanor) decided to found a monastery, he consulted Berno, the abbot of Baume.

<blockquote>He asked the latter about a site, but to his dismay the abbot fixed on Cluny, the favourite hunting-ground of the Duke, nay on the hunting-lodge itself. 'Impossible,' William replied, 'I cannot have my dogs removed.' Jocularly the abbot answered, 'Drive out the dogs, and put monks in their place, for thou canst well think what reward God will give thee for dogs, and what for monks.'<sup><a id="backlink-4" aria-label="Footnote #4" href="#footnote-4">[4]</a></sup></blockquote>

Berno, later St. Berno of Cluny, became the first abbot of Cluny and the teacher of St. Odo. But perhaps his greatest contribution, and likely the reason Cluny received the dedication of a modern classic, is that he persuaded a duke to donate his dog-filled hunting lodge to the Church. Without a doubt he appeared at the lodge at the commencement of construction to give the command, "Go, dogs. Go!"<sup><a id="backlink-5" aria-label="Footnote #5" href="#footnote-5">[5]</a></sup></blockquote>

## Pages 4-7: Dichotomy
In the Book of Genesis, chapter the first, we are first presented with a series of dichotomies: light and dark, the waters above and the waters beneath, land and sea. On these opening pages, Eastman gives us similar contrasts: "Big dogs and little dogs. Black and white dogs."

## Pages 8-9: Main Characters, First Appearance
The first chapter of Genesis ends with the creation of man and woman, and here Eastman gives us male and female. These dogs are the throughline of the plot. The female dog is wearing a hat with a flower and questions if the male dog approves. The male dog, himself hatless, does not approve, and they separate, albeit unacrimoniously. The flower, apparently a yellow daisy, is one that blooms in the spring, summer, and fall. Due to the male dog's lack of hat or other clothing, and other reasons that will become clear, we can conclude that this scene is set in summer.
