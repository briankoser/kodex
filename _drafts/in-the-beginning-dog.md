# In the Beginning, Dog: A Comedic Reading of P. D. Eastman's Go, Dog, Go!

## Dedication

Eastman dedicated his *bonum opus* "To Cluny". Some have claimed that Cluny was the name of Dr. Seuss's dog (source: my friend Ben texted it to me). That is probably the meaning of the dedication on the historical level, but that is merely the first, most base level of meaning. 

On a higher level, Cluny is a reference to the Abbey of Cluny, the largest church in Christendom until the building of St. Peter's Basilica. One of the most important monasteries in medieval Europe, Cluny led in the establishment of Western monasticism. It's influence waned as the pope's waxed, and it finally ceased to be a church when French Revolutionaries fired the buildings (including the library) and torn down the walls for stone to rebuild the town. The buildings that remain today house an engineering school. (source: my notes, I think from the online Encyclopedia Britannica but it may have been Wikipedia)

In addition to the abbey itself, Eastman may also (consciously, subconsciously, or unconsciously) have included one of the leaders of Cluny, in the same way that Shakespeare often refers to the king of England as "England" or to the king of France as "France". (source: The Complete Plays of Shakespeare) Three top candidates emerge.

Saint Odo of Cluny, the second abbot of Cluny, who is said to have invented the spoon. (source: ibid) The biggest argument against this theory is that the *Go, Dog, Go!* illustrations do not contain any spoons.

More likely is Odilo of Cluny, the fifth Benedictine abbot of Cluny. He made Cluny into the most important monastery in Western Europe and established All Souls' Day. (source: This one was probably Wikipedia) In support of this theory, there is a paper lantern on the boat on Page 33; this may or may not have been lit for the departed dead.

The most likely candidate is St. Berno of Cluny, Odo's teacher and the first abbot of Cluny. St. Berno wanted to reform Benedictine monasteries to adhere more closely to the Rule of St. Benedict. Duke William I of Aquitaine agreed to provide the money and land and asked St. Berno where the monastery should be founded. St. Berno asked for the duke's favorite hunting lodge in Cluny: a sacrifice for the duke. The Duke protested and said, "Impossible, I cannot have my dogs removed“. St. Berno answered, "Drive out the dogs and put monks in their place, for thou canst well think what reward God will give thee for dogs, and what for monks." (source: _The early history of the monastery of Cluny_, Oxford University Press, 1920). And so Cluny was built on the spot of the Duke's hunting lodge, which housed the Duke's dogs.
